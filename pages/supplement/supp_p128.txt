[p. 7] . . . Chaucer. . . did not write for specialists in his own
age, and his main value for succeeding ages resides not in his
vocabulary, nor in his inflections, nor in his indebtedness to
foreign originals, nor in the metrical uniformities or anomalies
that may be discovered in his poems; but in his poetry.
Other things are accidental; his poetry is essential. Other
interests—historical, philological, antiquarian—must be recog-
nized; but the poetical, or (let us say) the spiritual, interest
stands first and far ahead of all others. By virtue of it
Chaucer, now as always, makes his chief and his convincing
[p. 8] appeal to that which is spiritual in men.  ...
