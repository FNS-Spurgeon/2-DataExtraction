[After great praise of the excellence of the text, Macaulay
examines two points : (1) who is 'Lollius'? whom Chaucer
says he mainly follows in 'Troilus,' and (2) was Chaucer, in
this poem, indebted to Guido delle Colonne at all? His
answer to (1) is that it is clear that by 'Lollius' Chaucer
means Boccaccio; and to that for this poem Chaucer drew
from Benôit de Sainte-More and not from Guido.]

[p. 360] It seems clear, upon reviewing the whole problem, that if
Chaucer used Marco Polo's narrative, he either carelessly or
intentionally confused all the features of the setting that could
possibly be confused, and retained not a single really charac-
teristic trait of any person, place or event. It is only by
twisting everything that any part of Chaucer's story can be
brought into relation with any part of Polo's...
