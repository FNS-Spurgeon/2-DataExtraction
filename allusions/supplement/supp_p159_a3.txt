[p. 27] The Middle Age was not, at all points, romantic...
Chaucer's temper, for instance, was by no means romantic.
That "good sense" which Dryden mentions as his promi¬
nent trait; that  "low tone" which Lowell praises in him, and
which keeps him close to the common ground of experience,
pervade his greatest work, the Canterbury Tales, with an
insistent realism. Chaucer's pages are picturesque with tourna¬
ment hunting-parties, baronial feasts, miracles of saints, feats of
magic; but they are solid, as well, with the everyday life of
fourteenth-century England. They have the naïveté and
garrulity which are marks of mediæval work, but not the
quaintness and grotesquerie which are held to be marks of
romantie work. Not archaic speech, but a certain mental
twist constitutes quaintness. Herbert and Fuller are quaint;
Blake is grotesque; Dunn and Charles Lamb are willfully
quaint, subtle, and paradoxical. But Chaucer is always
straight-grained, broad and natural.
