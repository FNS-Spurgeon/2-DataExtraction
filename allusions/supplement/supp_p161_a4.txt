1899. Mead, William E. Color in Old English Poetry, [in] Publications
Modern Language Association, Baltimore, 1899, vol. xiv (new

The list of Old English colors is at best a rather short one,
and its meagreness is the more striking as soon as we begin
to compare it with the richness of color that appears in
Chaucer, or the mediæval romances, or in Shakespeare. The
difference is seen not merely in the greater amount of color
used by the later poets, but in the greater vividness and
freshness with which the color-words are applied. Look for
a moment at Chaucer's Prologue, which contains 858 lines.
The color-words are indeed simple,—black, white, brown,
blue, green, grey, pers (sky-blue), red, yellow, but they are
deliberately employed for a picturesque effect, which is enhanced
by the use of comparisons, a device never used for this
purpose in Old English poetry. The Frankeleyne's beard
is as white as a daisy; a purse is as white as morning's
milk; the monk's neck is white as the fleur-de-lys.
