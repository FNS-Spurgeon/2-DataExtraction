[General description of the Franklin's Tale, with the point
that the story of Arviragus is given in the Historia Regum
Britanniœ of Geoffrey of Monmouth, before the time of Marie
de France, and suggestions as to how this fictitious history
got built up. The third part of the paper closely examines
the phraseology, incidents, and sentiments of Chaucer's narrative
in order to detect how much his version owes to the probable
Breton source. The conclusion is:
'We surely need no more evidence from the poems of
Marie de France... It must now be clear that the Franklin's
Tale, not only in fundamental theme, but also in minute de-
tail, shows so great similarity to the extant Breton lays that
there can be no doubt that Chaucer's assertion regarding the
source of his narrative is to be unhesitatingly accepted..."
(p. 432).
The fourth part discusses the oriental parallels to part of
the story, and shows that these do not destroy the possibility
of its source being a Breton lay, such lays being a very mixed
product. Studies of other romances form part of the evidence.
The fifth part of the paper discusses Chaucer's handling of
the theme and claims original work by him in connexion with
four developments:
1. The discussion of the cause of evil in the world, à propos
of the existence of the dangerous rocks on the Breton coast.
2. Astrological references.
3. The 'pleynt' of Aurelius to Apollo (303-51).
4. Dorigen's 'compleynt' to Fortune (627-728).]
