[p. 309] In thus dealing with his characters and his situations,
Chaucer was "the first of modern poets to tell an extended
story on a dramatic plan" ([Courthope’s] History of English
Poetry, i, 307). Here, in this right use of the term dramatic,
we have for the first time the full greatness of this poem
recognized. . .
[p. 310] The term "dramatic" that Mr. Courthope has used in
describing this poem is... just the term that expresses its
essential excellence. And yet, to be taken as true, this term
has to be used, not in its vulgar sense, but in its true and
scientific sense.  ... Chaucer's poem is not only the first
example in our literature of the story developed on the
dramatic plan. It is the discovery, and the brilliant applica-
tion, by the poet of the fourteenth century, of the principles
of literary art that form in the drama and the romance alike
the special glory of the nineteenth.
It is plain from passages in the poem itself, that Chaucer
has pondered deeply on the artistic problem of emotion and
action, that is, on the theory of human motives, and on the
connected problem of artistic construction.
[Chaucer's deterministic view of life, p. 311. Arrangement
of the poem into 50 dramatic scenes with link passages,
p. 312. Of these 50 scenes, 32 have the artistic structure
of the dialogue (p. 313), each progressive stage of the action
being attained by the action of mind on mind through dialogue
(p. 314). Nine monologues, four given to Criseyde, and five
to Troylus, in some of them the utterance of passion being
carried to the highest point of lyrical force, p. 314. Only two
trio scenes, Chaucer having an aversion to these, and using
them with sensuous imaginativeness to give brilliant pictures
of life and natural scenery, p. 315. Fourteen persons in the
action, six women and eight men; ten of whom are of
secondary importance, pp. 315-6. The poet's art concentrated
on four characters, p. 316. The main characters, pp. 316-18.
Criseyde the permanent centre of greatest interest, p. 318.
The five parts of the dramatic scheme; minute revelation of
motive in 37 scenes, the climax in a single scene, and the
results of the climax, Criseyde's degradation under the influ¬
ence of Diomed in twelve scenes: postponement of the climax
to within one-fourth of the end, pp. 318-9. In 266 lines the
protasis of the drama; then, in 5486 lines the fully developed
epitasis, from the scene in the temple, opening the action, to
the beginning of the climax-scene; next in 619 lines, climax
and solution of the dramatic problem; and in 1820 lines, the
fourth stage, the seduction of Criseyde and Death of Troylus;
last of all, in 50 lines, the soul of Troylus, looking down from
Paradise on the baseness of earthly life.]
